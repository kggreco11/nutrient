# Sports nutrition targets: evidence beyond standard reference intakes

Research checked 27 October 2026. This is a targeted evidence review, not a new systematic review. Context: male, provisionally age 26, 168 lb (76.2 kg), swimming four to five times weekly and lifting three times weekly; weight stable at roughly 3,000–3,200 kcal; wants gradual muscle gain and to track persistent nutrient gaps. Reports chronically low vitamin D. This report proposes discussion ranges; it does not silently change saved app targets or prescribe supplements.

## What the evidence supports

The useful distinction is between a population reference intake, a higher intake supported by biomarkers, an intake shown to improve performance, and a dose used to correct deficiency. They are different numbers. Athlete consensus recognizes increased turnover/losses and uncertain optimal status, while emphasizing assessment of actual intake and nutrient status. It does not establish a blanket multiplier for all micronutrients. [IOC consensus, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027).

Some newer evidence does justify looking beyond the U.S. numbers, especially B12. For many other micronutrients, trials do not establish a higher athletic or longevity target. A January 2026 sports-vitamin review still identifies the need for better sport-specific dosing studies; it is a narrative review rather than a meta-analysis. [Wiacek et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41599826/).

## B12 and B6 deserve a more substantive answer

**B12:** In 299 healthy adults aged 18–50, an observational study found B12-related biomarkers appeared to plateau around **4–7 µg/day**. This is relevant to the user's age but measures biochemical status, not muscle growth, swimming speed, or survival. [Bor et al., AJCN 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20071646/). A dose-response meta-analysis also relates intake to B12 status, without identifying a performance optimum. [Dullemeijer et al., AJCN 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23269815/).

A peer-reviewed 2023 scoping review considers approximately **5–7 µg/day** adequate based on biomarkers, while acknowledging no consensus on an optimal metabolic profile. The formal Nordic 2023 AI is **4 µg/day**, versus the U.S. RDA of 2.4 µg/day. A reasonable discussion option is therefore a **4–7 µg/day dietary planning band**, clearly labeled as a biomarker-based band rather than a proven athletic optimum. [Bjørke-Monsen and Lysne, 2023](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/10257), [Nordic B12 reference and evidence gaps](https://pub.norden.org/nord2023-003/vitamin-b12.html).

**B6:** The question about adequacy beyond older thresholds is legitimate. A 2023 peer-reviewed scoping review questions whether the traditional plasma PLP sufficiency threshold is adequate, using a functional kynurenine metabolite ratio. These biochemical observations do not establish an optimal oral dose or show that increasing intake improves hypertrophy. [Bjørke-Monsen and Ueland, 2023](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/10259). The older sport-specific review suggests exercise may increase B6 and riboflavin needs but does not validate a general high-dose target. [Woolf and Manore, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17240780/).

The contemporary Nordic male B6 reference is **1.8 mg/day**, compared with the U.S. 1.3 mg/day. **1.8 mg/day is a defensible alternate comparator** for the app; a 2 mg food-planning target would be a practical rounding choice, not a trial-proven optimum. [Nordic B6 reference](https://pub.norden.org/nord2023-003/files/691f2f1d301c2_vitamin-b6.pdf). EFSA's separate 2023 risk review established **12 mg/day as the adult upper limit**, based on peripheral neuropathy. That is a ceiling for habitual total intake, not a target. Use the EFSA opinion for the limit: the linked Nordic chapter contains a discrepant 12.5 mg statement. [EFSA scientific opinion, 2023](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8006).

A small positive 2023 crossover trial deserves mention: 32 non-athletes aged 20–30 took a multi-ingredient B-complex product for 28 days and improved a running-to-exhaustion endpoint. It cannot attribute the effect to B6 or B12, establish a single-vitamin requirement, or demonstrate long-term safety or benefit in trained swimmers/lifters. It is hypothesis-generating, not a basis for a high-dose daily score. [B-complex randomized trial, 2023](https://www.medsci.org/v20p1272.htm).

## Why Thorne's amounts are much higher

The current **Basic Nutrients 2/Day** official label lists **20 mg B6 as P5P and 600 µg B12 as methylcobalamin per two capsules**. Thorne describes the formula as gap-filling nutritional support and emphasizes bioavailable forms. Its accessible product text does **not** establish that those precise amounts maximize athletic performance or healthspan. NSF Certified for Sport concerns product testing, label compliance, and banned substances; it is not proof that a dose is optimal. [Thorne official label and FAQ, checked 27 October 2026](https://www.thorne.com/products/dp/basic-nutrients-2-day?hl=en-IN).

B12 absorption provides a physiological reason high oral doses can be useful: active uptake saturates, while a small fraction is absorbed passively. This explains how a large pill dose can deliver a much smaller absorbed amount, especially when absorption is impaired. It does not establish Thorne's formulation intent or mean 600 µg is a dietary requirement. [BOND B12 review, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6297555/). Do not generalize this rationale to B6: the labeled 20 mg exceeds the newer EFSA 12 mg/day adult limit before food is counted.

An older Thorne article titled “Why Are Ingredient Amounts In Supplements So Much Higher…” exists, but its article body was unavailable in the accessible response during this review. No claims about its explanation are attributed to it.

## Implications for the app

- Preserve both reference intake and user-selected target. Display which is being scored; don't imply every target has identical evidence.
- Distinguish known intake below the chosen target from missing food composition. Trace-mineral values are frequently absent; absent chromium is not zero chromium.
- Show food and supplemental contributions separately, and average intake across recorded days. One incomplete day cannot establish chronic insufficiency.
- Keep B6 upper-limit comparisons separate from adequacy percentages. An intake of 20 mg should not earn a larger nutrient score than adequate intake.
- Consider the B12 4–7 µg band and B6 1.8 mg comparator with the user before changing saved targets. The remaining table entries do not justify blanket upward revisions.
- Intake logs cannot show whether chronic low vitamin D or iron/B12 deficiency has resolved. A future optional lab-history feature would answer a different question from daily nutrient coverage.

## Remaining uncertainty

This review does not establish a complete optimal-longevity micronutrient formula. Direct lifespan or healthspan trials in healthy young athletes are sparse. It also does not establish individual requirements for sodium replacement, separate ALA/EPA/DHA targets, creatine, or training-day hydration; those require separate evidence review and should not be folded into a generic “more nutrients = better” score.
